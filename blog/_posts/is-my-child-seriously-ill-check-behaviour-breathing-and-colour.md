When my eldest daughter was four, I tookher to the GP twice. Both times I was told it was a tummy bug. Both times I went home with a nagging feeling that something wasn't right.

It turned out to be E. coli O157. She spent1 0 days in hospital on fluids, and we were very lucky: in around 15% of cases it can cause acute kidney failure. To this day we don't know how she caught it.

I work for the NHS and teach first aid, and I still doubted myself. What I learned is that a name for the illness matters less than how your child *looks and acts*. So the advice I give to parents now is to check three things: behaviour, breathing and body colour.

## 1. Behaviour: are they "not themselves"?

You know your child better than any thermometer does. Worrying signs include:

•      Floppy or limp, like a rag doll

•      Unusually sleepy, hard to wake, or drifting off mid-conversation

•      Not interested in anything, even favourite toys, snacks or cuddles

•      Not responding to you, avoiding eye contact, or not smiling when they normally would

•      A high-pitched, weak or moaning cry, or crying that can't be soothed

•      Confused or "not making sense"

A poorly child who still plays for a few minutes is usually reassuring. A child who stays flat and glassy-eyed is not.

## 2. Breathing: what does it look and sound like?

Take their top off and watch their chest and tummy for a full minute. Look and listen for:

•      Fast breathing that isn't settling, even when they're calm

•      Skin pulling in between or under the ribs, or at the base of the neck

•      Nostrils flaring with each breath

•      Grunting at the end of each breath

•      Noisy breathing, such as wheezing or a harsh whistling sound

•      Struggling to speak, feed or drink because they can't catch their breath

Trouble breathing can get worse quickly in little ones, so don't wait and see.

## 3. Body colour: what is their skin telling you?

Colour is one of the clearest warning signs, and it's easy to check:

•      Pale, grey, blotchy or mottled skin

•      Blue or greyish lips, tongue or face

•      Cold hands and feet along with looking unwell

•      A rash that doesn't fade when you press a clear glass firmly against it

On darker skin, colour changes can be harder to spot on the face. Check the tongue, inside the lips, the gums, the palms of the hands and soles of the feet, and look for rashes onlighter areas of skin.

## Trust your instincts

If you have a feeling that something is wrong, that's useful information, not fuss. If the first appointment doesn't settle your worry, go back or call 111. Try saying: "I'm worried my child is seriously unwell, and they're not acting like themselves."

## When to get help

Call 999 (or go to A&E) if your child:

•      Is struggling to breathe, grunting, or has blue or grey lips or skin

•      Is limp, floppy, or very hard to wake

•      Has a rash that doesn't fade under a glass and seems unwell

•      Has a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or has their first ever seizure

Call 111 or your GP urgently if yourchild:

•      Is breathing faster than usual, or looks pale and drowsy

•      Has signs of dehydration, such as fewer wet nappies, a dry mouth or no tears

•      Has bloody diarrhoea or severe tummy pain

•      Just doesn't seem right and you can't say why

Not sure which to choose? Call 111. That's what they're there for.

## A final word

You will never be "wasting anyone's time" by checking on your child. I'm so glad and trusted my gut instinct and called an ambulance in the end, and I hope this helps you feel more confident doing the same.
